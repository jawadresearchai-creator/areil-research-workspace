ABSTRACT
Plant Class III peroxidases have diverse roles in controlling root hair growth, anther development, and 
abiotic and biotic stress responses. However, their abiotic stress response mechanism in rice remains 
elusive. Here, we identified a peroxidase precursor gene, OsPRX83, and investigated its role in enhancing 
osmotic stress tolerance in rice. We used OsPRX83 overexpression and CRISPR-Cas9-generated mutant 
lines to elucidate OsPRX83‘s function and expression patterns under stress conditions. The expression of 
OsPRX83 was induced by H2O2, PEG, NaCl, and ABA treatments. Using qRT-PCR, RNA sequencing, and 
physiological assays, we demonstrated that overexpression of OsPRX83 enhanced the osmotic and 
oxidative stress tolerance as compared to the wild-type and mutant seedlings, as evident from the higher 
survival rates, enhanced peroxidase (POD) and ascorbate peroxidase (APX) activities, and increased ABA 
sensitivity compared with mutants and wild-type plants. Transcriptome analysis further supported the 
involvement of OsPRX83 in the ROS scavenging, by modulating the expression of OsDREB1B, OsDREB1E, 
OsDREB1F, OsDREB1G in response to osmotic treatment. In summary, our study suggests that OsPRX83 
plays a pivotal role in enhancing stress tolerance in rice through ABA-dependent pathways and ROS 
scavenging. Therefore, this study elucidates the function of a novel abiotic stress response gene in rice, 
thereby may contribute to a new genetic engineering resource for engineering drought-resistant rice 
varieties.

Introduction
Rice, a staple food for over half the global population1, is 
highly vulnerable to drought stress due to its substantial 
water requirements, especially amid global climate change. 
Plants have developed various strategies, including mor-
phological and physiological adaptations to counteract 
water deficiency and mitigate drought stress.2,3 Abscisic 
acid (ABA), a crucial plant hormone, is pivotal in 
responding to drought and salinity stresses.4,5 During 
drought stress, ABA-dependent and ABA-independent 
pathways activate diverse genes, including transcription 
factors. Many drought response genes are implicated in 
ABA-dependent pathways. Notably, the bZIP transcription 
factor OsABF1 directly regulated the expression of the 
protein phosphatase 2C (OsPP108 and OsPP48), forming 
a complex feedback loop through ABA signaling 
pathways.6 The PP2C and PYR1-like (PYL) proteins con-
stituted ABA receptors in Arabidopsis thaliana.7,8 
Overexpression of OsPYL3 in Arabidopsis has been 
shown to enhance cold and drought stress tolerance. 9 
Multiple members of the cyclic nucleotide-gated channels 
(CNGC) family served as ABA-activated Ca2+ channels in 
Arabidopsis guard cells. Specifically, CNGC5, CNGC6, 
CNGC9, and CNGC12 have played crucial roles in the 
ABA-specific patterns of cytosolic Ca2+ signaling 
pathways.10 To elucidate the mechanisms of ABA- 
induced regulation of CNGCs and Ca2+ signaling, 
researchers have discovered that OST1 physically inter-
acted with CNGC5/6/9/12. Phosphorylation sites targeted 
by OST1 have been identified in CNGC5, CNGC6, 
CNGC9, and CNGC12. These phosphorylation sites are 
essential for ABA-induced activation of Ca2+ oscillations.11
Plants respond to stress through intricate networks, one of 
which involves the participation of signal molecules such as 
reactive oxygen species (ROS) in the ABA signal pathways.12 
ROS exhibit a dual role in plant cells. They can be toxic 
byproducts of aerobic metabolism at high concentrations, but 
also toxic in a control manner during oxidative burst. At lower 
concentrations, they participate in signal transduction.13 To 
maintain cellular balance, plants have evolved diverse strategies 
for regulating ROS production, transport, and scavenging.14 
Numerous proteins, notably superoxide dismutase (SOD), cat-
alase (CAT), ascorbate peroxidase (APX), glutathione perox-
idase (GPX), ascorbic acid, glutathione (GSH), and peroxidase 
(PRX), are integral to ROS detoxification.15 Among these, 
peroxidases are particularly crucial in oxidoreduction reac-
tions, employing hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) as the electron 
acceptor in microorganisms, plants, and animals.16

In the living organism, three peroxidase classes exist: Class 
I (ascorbate peroxidases), Class II (lignin peroxidases in certain 
fungi), and Class III (secretory peroxidases, Prxs).17 Among 
them, Class III peroxidases, specifically, contribute to stress 
tolerance,18 lignin biosynthesis,19 and root hair growth in 
Arabidopsis.20 Arabidopsis thaliana and Oryza sativa possess 
73 and 138 Prx genes, respectively,21,22 although the functions 
of most remain undetermined. In rice, MYBS1 regulated two 
Class III peroxidases by binding to the bsr-d1 promoter with 
a single base change, enhancing resistance to Magnaporthe 
oryzae.23 Additionally, OsPrx30, a Class III peroxidase in rice, 
localized in the endoplasmic reticulum, impacted rice resis-
tance against Xanthomonas oryzae pv. oryzae (Xoo) when 
OsATH1 binded to its promoter, by scavenging ROS.24 
Furthermore, OsPRX38, another Class III peroxidase, 
improved arsenic tolerance by increasing antioxidase activities 
upon overexpression.25
Despite Class III peroxidases having diverse roles in various 
biological processes, the molecular mechanisms of how Prxs 
respond to osmotic stress in rice remain unknown. Despite the 
size of this family, the functions of most members, particularly 
in response to abiotic stresses, remain largely unknown. 
Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the role of 
OsPRX83, a Class III peroxidase gene, in response to osmotic 
stress in rice. To achieve this objective, we used a combination 
of gene expression analysis and functional characterization 
through overexpression and knockout studies. Understanding 
the function of OsPRX83 in osmotic stress response will pro-
vide new insights into the complex regulatory networks 
involved in abiotic stress tolerance in rice, which has important 
implications for the development of stress-tolerant rice 
varieties.
Materials and methods
Plasmid construction and rice transformation
The full-length coding region of OsPRX83, derived from Oryza 
sativa japonica cv. Nipponbare cDNA, was cloned into the 
pCXUN-FLAG vector26 to generate OsPRX83 overexpression 
(OE) constructs driven by the ubiquitin promoter. For the 
OsPRX83 CRISPR/Cas9 vector, a sgRNA sequence was 
designed using the Rice Information GateWay (RIGW, http:// 
rice.hzau.edu.cn/rice_rs3/).27 This sequence was then cloned 
into the pU3-sgRNA vector and subsequently introduced into 
the pH-Ubi-cas9-7 vector via a gateway cloning LR reaction 
(ThermoFisher Scientific). For expression pattern analysis, the 
OsPRX83 promoter region (2 kb fragment) was inserted into 
the pCXGUS-P vector, driving the β-glucuronidase (GUS) 
reporter gene expression. Agrobacterium tumefaciens EH105 
received these vectors, which were then transferred into 
Nipponbare callus. Transgenic homozygous lines in the T3 
generation were utilized for further study. Table S1 lists all 
primers used for plasmid construction.
Plant materials and stress treatments
This study utilized the well-established Oryza sativa japo-
nica cv. Nipponbare as the study material.28 To investigate 
OsPRX83‘s expression profile under diverse abiotic stresses 
and phytohormone treatments, wild type (wt) four-leaf 
seedlings were subjected to diverse treatments, including 
cold (4°C), drought (20% PEG 4000, w/v), salt (150 mM 
NaCl), and oxidative (1% H2O2) stress.29 Stressed leaf and 
shoot samples were collected at 0, 1, 3, 6, 12, and 24 h post- 
treatment, with additional samples for cold stress at 36 and 
48 h. Phytohormone treatments involved soaking seedlings 
in 1/2 Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium containing 100  
μM of ABA, GA, MeJA, and IAA, respectively. Root sam-
ples were obtained at 0 h, 15 min, 1 h, 3 h, 6 h, 12 h, and 
24 h.
To assess the osmotic and oxidative stress tolerance of rice 
seedlings, seeds of OsPRX83-OE, mutants and wt were initially 
grown on solid 1/2 MS medium for 3–4 days, then transferred to 
1/2 MS medium (control), 120 mM and 150 mM mannitol-D, 
and 1 μM and 3 μM Methyl viologen (MV) for oxidative stress.30 
Following cultivation in a growth chamber for 10 days under 
a 16-h light/8-h dark cycle at 28°C, the shoot and root lengths 
were measured, and seedling phenotypes were documented.
For drought resistance analysis in OsPRX83 transgenic 
plants, three-leaf seedlings of OsPRX83 transgenic and wt 
plants (15 plants per line) were grown in large plastic boxes 
filled with well-mixed soil and cultivated in a growth chamber 
for two weeks. The water deprivation treatments ensued for 
10–12 days until all seedling leaves were wilted, followed by 
a 20-day water recovery period to calculate survival rates. The 
ABA sensitivity assay was conducted31 by transferring germi-
nated seeds of OsPRX83 transgenic lines and wt to 1/2 MS 
medium containing 1 μM ABA, 3 μM ABA, or no ABA (con-
trol). After 10 days of cultivation, seedling phenotypes were 
recorded, and relative shoot height and root length were mea-
sured for all plants, with each test performed in triplicates.
Physiological measurements
The proline and chlorophyll contents were assessed pre- and 
post-treatment with 120 mM mannitol for three days. Leaf 
samples from OsPRX83 transgenic lines and wt were obtained 
using the method previously described.32 For reactive oxygen 
detection, detached leaves from each genotype (minimum 
three plants per genotype) were immersed in a 0.5 mg/ml 
NBT staining solution. Following vacuum application for 30  
min, the leaves underwent staining for 24 h. Subsequent deco-
lorization involved 95% ethanol in an 80°C water bath until the 
leaves lost green coloration. Images were captured using 
a Leica M165FC stereoscope. Ascorbate peroxidase (APX) 
and peroxidase (POD) activities were quantified by 
Peroxidase (POD) Assay Kit (for plants) and Ascorbate 
Peroxidase (APX) Assay Kit according to the manufacturer’s 
guidelines (Nanjing Jian Cheng Bioengineering Institute). The 
principle of the Plant Peroxidase (POD) Assay Kit is based on 
the catalytic reaction of peroxidase with hydrogen peroxide, 
the enzyme activity is determined by measuring the absorbance 
change at 420 nm. For the principle of the APX Assay Kit, APX 
catalyzes the reaction between ascorbic acid (ASA) and hydro-
gen peroxide (H2O2), converting ASA to monodehydroascor-
bic acid (MDASA). As AsA is oxidized, the absorbance value at 
290 nm wavelength in the solution decreases. The ascorbate

β-Glucuronidase (GUS) staining analysis
Different tissues from pOsPRX83: GUS transgenic rice, includ-
ing roots, stems, leaves, and spikelets were collected and pro-
cessed using modified methods based on Jefferson.33 The 
samples were initially soaked in 90% acetone for 30 minutes 
on ice, then washed three times in a buffer solution (2 mM 
potassium ferricyanide, 2 mM potassium ferrocyanide, 0.2% 
Triton-X-100 in 0.2 M PBS, pH 7.2) with gentle shaking for 
five minutes per wash. Following this, the samples underwent 
staining in a solution (buffer solution +20 mg/ml X-Gluc) for 
48 h at 37°C. Finally, the stained samples were photographed 
using a stereoscope (Leica M165FC).
RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR
Total RNA was isolated from various tissues of OsPRX83 
transgenic lines and wild-type rice using the RNA Extraction 
Kit (Promega). The Reverse Transcription System Kit 
(Promega) was then employed to synthesize first-strand 
cDNA. Quantitative RT-PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed on 
an ABI Step One Real-Time PCR system (Applied Biosystems) 
using the Hieff qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (Yeasen). The 
reactions were conducted under the following conditions: 
initial denaturation at 95°C for 5 minutes, followed by 40 cycles 
of denaturation at 95°C for 10 seconds and annealing/exten-
sion at 60°C for 30 seconds, culminating in the stage of melting 
curve analysis. Samples from the wild-type or untreated wild- 
type tissue were chosen as controls. The relative gene expres-
sion levels were calculated with the 2−∆∆Ct method,34 employ-
ing the rice ubiquitin3 gene as an endogenous control.35 All 
data analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 8.0.2. The 
primers used for gene expression analysis are listed in Table S1.
RNA sequencing analyses
At the four-leaf stage, OsPRX83 overexpression lines, mutants, 
and wt seedlings were treated with 120 mM mannitol for three 
days. Total RNA was extracted from the leaves before and after 
treatment, with three biological replicates per sample, using the 
previously described method. The purified RNA samples were 
sent to Gene Denovo Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou, 
China) for cDNA library preparation using the Illumina HiSeq 
2500 platform. The clean reads were then aligned to the Rice 
Annotation Project Database (RAP-DB). Differentially expressed 
genes (DEGs) were performed by DESeq236 software and shown 
in the Table S2. They were identified with a threshold of log2 (fold 
change) ≥ 1 (False Discovery Rate – FDR < 0.05).37 Gene 
Ontology (GO) enrichment and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes 
and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analyses38,39 were performed 
based on the DEGs data using the website (https://www.omics 
mart.com/). Certain DEGs that are upregulated in overexpression 
(OE) lines and downregulated in mutants are chosen and vali-
dated using qRT-PCR.
Statistical analysis
All data analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0.2. All 
datasets from the overexpression lines, mutants, and wild type 
(wt) were evaluated using IBM SPSS Statistics 19. Values at the 
p < 0.05 indicated statistical significance. Different Letters indicate 
significant differences at p < 0.05 by one-way analysis of Variance 
(ANOVA)40 and Duncan’s multiple range post hoc test.
Results
Expression patterns of OsPRX83
The expression levels of OsPRX83 in various tissues were 
examined using qRT-PCR to investigate its function. The 
results indicated high expression in roots, but low expression 
in leaves, stems, and spikelets (Figure 1(a)). This expression 
profile was corroborated by a GUS staining assay, which 
showed a strong GUS signal in roots (Figure 1a) and 
a relatively weak signal in stems, leaves, and spikelets 
(Figure 1b-e). To further explore the expression patterns 
under different abiotic stresses and phytohormone treatments, 
a time course assay was conducted using qRT-PCR. The 
expression level of OsPRX83 peaked at 24 h under cold treat-
ment (Figure S1 a), but was down-regulated under heat treat-
ment (Figure S1 b). In 1% H2O2 and ABA treatments, 
OsPRX83 expression significantly increased at 1 h, followed 
by a decrease (Figure S1 c, f). High salt treatment significantly 
induced OsPRX83 expression (Figure S1 d), while the expres-
sion pattern under PEG treatment mirrored that of oxidative 
treatment (Figure S1 e). GA treatment up-regulated OsPRX83 
expression between 30 min and 3 h, followed by a decrease 
(Figure S1 g). Finally, under MeJA treatment, the expression 
level peaked at 1 h (Figure S1 h), remaining constant thereafter 
during the time course.
Molecular characterization of OsPRX83 transgenic plant
To examine OsPRX83‘s function under abiotic stress, we gen-
erated OsPRX83 mutants using the CRISPR/Cas9 genome edit-
ing system. During the T0 generation, DNA of shoot tissues of 
transgenic seedlings was extracted, then designed primers to 
sequence the CRISPR-Cas9 knockout sites by designing speci-
fic primers, compared the sequencing results with the wild 
type. Two homozygous mutant lines, cas9-4B1 and 4C1, each 
with 4 bp deletions, were selected (Figure 2(a)). Additionally, 
we generated OE transgenic lines using the ubiquitin promo-
ter. qRT-PCR analysis revealed a significantly increased 
expression levels in OE-7 and OE-12 (32-fold and 218-fold, 
respectively) compared to the wt (Figure 2(b)). Subsequent 
investigations utilized T3 homozygous transgenic lines.

Overexpression of OsPRX83 increased ABA sensitivity in 
rice
OsPRX83 expression significantly increased after a 15-min ABA 
treatment (Supplementary Figure S1 f). To probe OsPRX83‘s role 
in ABA response, we conducted ABA sensitivity assays using 
OsPRX83 transgenic lines, including OE lines (OE) and mutants. 
After a 10-day ABA treatment (0, 1, and 3 µM), OE lines exhibited 
significantly suppressed seedling growth compared to wt and 
mutants (Figure 4(a), Figure S4 a), suggesting that OsPRX83-OE 
heightens ABA sensitivity during the seedling stage. Furthermore, 
OsPRX83-OE seedlings displayed significantly shorter relative 
shoot lengths than wt and mutants (Figure 4(b), Figure S4 b). 
These findings were corroborated by assessing relative root length 
(Figure 4(c) and S4 c).
To explore the potential link between OsPRX83 expression 
and ABA response, we examined the expression levels of sev-
eral ABA response genes via qRT-PCR. OsPP108 expression41 
was significantly upregulated in OsPRX83-OE lines following 
ABA treatment for 30 min and 1 h, relative to wt and mutants 
(Figure S5 a). Additionally, PYL3 expression9 peaked at 30 min 
in the OE lines before decreasing (Figure S5 b).Overall, these 
findings suggest that OsPRX83 may participate in ABA signal-
ing pathways by modulating OsPP108 and PYL3 expression.
Overexpression of OsPRX83 improved oxidative stress 
resistance in rice
OsPRX83 encodes a Class III peroxidase precursor, with expres-
sion induced by H2O2 within one hour (Fig. S1 c). To investigate 
OsPRX83‘s role in oxidative stress response, we cultivated germi-
nated seeds of OsPRX83-OE, wt, and mutant plants on 1/2 MS 
medium supplemented with 0, 1, or 3 μM MV. After 10 days, MV 
treatment suppressed shoot growth in all seedlings, yet OsPRX83- 
OE plants displayed longer shoots than wt and mutants (Figure 5 
(a) and S6 a). This observation was corroborated by relative shoot 
length measurements (Figure 5(b) and S6 b). Furthermore, the 
fresh weight per plant significantly declined post-treatment, but 
OsPRX83-OE plants showed less oxidative damage than wt and 
mutants (Figure 5(c) and S6 c). Thus, OsPRX83 overexpression in 
rice conferred reduced growth inhibition and enhanced oxidative 
stress tolerance.

Transcriptome profiling of OsPRX83 transgenic lines
To further elucidate the molecular function of OsPRX83, 
a transcriptome analysis was performed on overexpression 
lines OE-12, wt, and cas9-4C1 mutants.
Compared with the wild type, the overexpression line OE- 
12 displayed an upregulation of 604 genes and a down- 
regulation of 314 genes. Conversely, the cas9-4C1 mutants 
revealed an upregulation of 923 genes and a down-regulation 
of 600 genes. Upon subjecting the OE-12 line to a three-day 
mannitol treatment, 5087 genes were up-regulated and 2469 
genes were down-regulated, while the cas9-4C1 mutants exhib-
ited upregulation of 3349 genes and down-regulation of 1070 
genes. Notably, a greater number of both up-regulated and 
down-regulated genes were observed in OE-12 compared to 
cas9-4C1 under mannitol treatment, as indicated by the vol-
cano map (Figure 7(a)). Figure 7(b) illustrates the analysis of 
DEGs across the various lines. The comparison between the wt 
and the OE-12 line yielded 104 DEGs, while that between the 
wt and the cas9-4C1 mutants resulted in 320 DEGs. 
Additionally, 564 DEGs were identified when comparing the 
wt and OE-12-T (under treatment), and 297 DEGs were found 
between the wt and cas9-4C1-T (under treatment). Among 
these DEGs, certain genes exhibited upregulation in OE-12 
and down-regulation in cas9-4C1, with the wild type showing 
intermediate expression levels. A heat map (Figure 7(c)) was 
constructed to visualize the expression patterns of these 
selected DEGs. Additionally, the expression of four genes was 
confirmed using qRT-PCR (Figure 7(d)). Previous reports 
suggested that OsDREB1B, OsDREB1E, OsDREB1F, and 
OsDREB1G, which are implicated in abiotic stress 
responses,42 may have relevant biological functions. These 
transcriptome data suggest a potential role for OsPRX83 in 
abiotic stress responses.

development like control root hair growth20 and promote root 
hair growth at low temperature43 and abiotic stress 
responses.44 However, the underlying molecular mechanism 
of their abiotic stress responses remains largely unknown. For 
example, overexpression of AtPrx3 enhanced plant salt and 
drought tolerance.45 Similarly, overexpression of two class III 
peroxidase genes (CrPrx and CrPrx1) in Chrysanthemum 
showed increased germination rates in CrPrx1-overexpressing 
lines as compared to wild type and CrPrx overexpressing lines 
under drought and salt stress conditions.46 Overexpression of 
the Arabidopsis gene AtPrx64 in tobacco also enhanced plant 
aluminum stress tolerance.47 Overexpression of the GsPRX9 in 
soybean was found to confer salt stress tolerance in transgenic 
seedlings.48 Although the rice genome contains 138 Class III 
peroxidases, with two members, OsPRX30 and OsPRX38, hav-
ing reported functions.24,25 Phylogenetic analysis of the Prx 
family in rice is categorized into eight groups. OsPRX30, 
OsPRX38, OsPRX83 belong to three subgroup of Prx family 
in rice, respectively.24 Similarly, the roles of OsPRX30 and 
OsPRX38 are related with stresses responses, the function of 
OsPRX83 may be associate with stress response as well.
In our study, the OsPRX83 expression was induced by 
H2O2, NaCl, PEG, and ABA treatments, which led us to 
analyze its tolerance under these abiotic stress conditions 
using OsPRX83 transgenic and wt plants. To further con-
firm our hypothesis, we found that OsPRX83 overexpres-
sion enhanced tolerance to osmotic stress, via mannitol 
treatment, and oxidative stress, via MV treatment 
(Figures 3 and 5). Moreover, the OsPRX83 overexpression 
augmented osmotic stress tolerance by boosting antioxidant 
reductase activities, specifically POD and APX (Figure 6). 
Therefore, we demonstrated OsPRX83‘s positive regulatory 
role in enhancing osmotic stress resistance through 
enhanced ROS scavenging. Like OsPRX30 and OsPRX38, 
OsPRX83 is also considered as stress response gene through 
ROS metabolic pathways.
Moreover, the qRT-PCR analysis of RNA-seq data revealed the 
expression levels of OsDREB1B, OsDREB1E, OsDREB1F, and 
OsDREB1G (Figure 7d). These DREB transcription factors are 
known for their roles in abiotic stress response in rice. 
Specifically, OsDREB1B overexpression enhanced the cold and 
high salt stress tolerance. Moreover, OsDREB1E and OsDREB1G 
overexpression increased water deficit stress tolerance,49 while 
OsDREB1F overexpression improved the tolerance against 
drought, salt, and cold stresses in rice and Arabidopsis.50 
Additionally, OsATH1, an AT-hook transcription factor, binded 
to the OsPRX30 promoter, thereby regulating ROS 
accumulation.24 Therefore, we hypothesize that OsDREBs may 
bind to the promoter of OsPRX83. To confirm this, OsDREB1B, 
OsDREB1E, OsDREB1F, and OsDREB1G are potential candidate 
genes for interaction with OsPRX83, requiring validation through 
yeast one-hybrid and Co-IP assays.
Also, OsPRX83 (LOC_Os06g32990) also has a homologous 
gene (LOC_Os06g35490), which is also an unknown functional 
gene. Some genes have been found to interact with their homo-
logous genes. The RNA binding proteins OsLa1 and OsLa2 are 
homologs in rice, and they interacted with together to regulate 
the pollen fertility and grain size.51 OsPRX83 can also be 
interacted with its homologs in rice.
Compared with prior findings, our study elucidates the 
role of OsPRX83 in modulating tolerance to osmotic stress 
through reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenging path-
ways and abscisic acid (ABA) signaling pathways. The 
interacting proteins of OsPRX83 implicated in this regula-
tory mechanism remains to be determined. Future investi-
gations aim to unveil the intricate regulatory networks 
underlying the response to osmotic stress in rice, thereby 
enriching our understanding of the involvement of plant 
peroxidases in stress response mechanisms.
Conclusion
This study elucidates a novel Class III peroxidase gene in rice, 
OsPRX83. Utilizing overexpression and CRISPR-Cas9 knock-
down mutants, we established OsPRX83‘s role in responding to 
abiotic stresses. Our findings suggest that OsPRX83 overexpres-
sion bolsters osmotic stress tolerance by augmenting ROS scaven-
ging. Furthermore, OsPRX83 positively regulates responses to 
oxidative stress and ABA sensitivity. This finding underscores 
the importance of ROS metabolism under abiotic stresses, thereby 
unveiling a new dimension in the functional analysis of plant 
peroxidases.
